## Plain-English definition

The part of a wall that extends above the roof line — the low wall you see around the
edge of most flat-roofed commercial buildings. The International Building Code defines
it simply as "the part of any wall entirely above the roofline."

## Why it matters to you

**Verified:** Parapets are where the roof system, the wall system, and the weather all
meet, and building-enclosure specialists single them out because the water, air, and
thermal control layers of two different assemblies must connect there without a gap
([IIBEC Interface — parapets](https://iibec.org/publication-post/parapets-the-critical-juncture-between-roofs-and-walls/)).
Unlike an ordinary wall, a parapet is exposed to the weather on both sides, and gaps in
its air barrier can pull moist air into the assembly and cause condensation damage that
shows up as a "roof leak"
([GAF — parapet control layers](https://www.gaf.com/en-us/blog/building-science/parapets-part-1-continuity-of-control-layers-9c3932f7-8144-4565-8ffd-b840f7088c0f)).
For an owner, that means parapet copings, base [flashings](/glossary/flashing/), and
counterflashings deserve specific attention in every
[inspection](/knowledge/commercial-roof-inspections/) — a stained parapet wall is a
finding, not a cosmetic issue.

## Technical detail

Good parapet practice runs top-down: a coping cap sloped back toward the roof so water
sheds onto the membrane rather than down the building face; membrane base flashing
turned up the parapet and terminated with flashing/counterflashing detailed to allow
differential movement between roof and wall; and continuous water, air, thermal, and
vapor control layers carried across the transition. Parapets also interact with wind
design — a flush roof edge with no parapet provides the least wind-uplift protection at
the edge, and perimeter edge metal and copings are covered by their own securement
standard (ANSI/SPRI/FM 4435/ES-1).

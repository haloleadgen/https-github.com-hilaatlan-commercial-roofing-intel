## How a roof claim is actually evaluated

An adjuster's file answers three questions: What was the roof's condition before the
event? What damage is attributable to the event? Did the insured meet their post-loss
duties? Owners lose value on claims not because damage wasn't real, but because they
can't evidence the first question or didn't perform the third.

## The sequence

1. **Notice.** Report promptly per the policy — late notice is a coverage defense.
2. **Mitigate and document.** Temporary protection to stop ongoing damage, with receipts
   and photos. Mitigation is a duty; unmitigated worsening may not be covered.
3. **Independent damage assessment.** Before or alongside the carrier's inspection, get
   your own qualified assessment (roofer or engineer, depending on stakes). Moisture
   surveys distinguish new intrusion from old saturation — powerful, objective evidence.
4. **Adjuster inspection.** Attend it. Walk the roof with them, with your file.
5. **Scope negotiation.** Disputes are usually about scope (repair vs. replace, matching,
   code upgrades) rather than coverage. This is where documentation pays.
6. **Settlement and repair.** Only now do permanent repairs proceed — under
   code-compliant scope. See [Repair or Replace](/knowledge/guides/repair-or-replace/).

## Wind damage vs. wear-and-tear: the core dispute

Policies cover sudden storm damage, not gradual deterioration. Adjusters are trained to
attribute damage to age where the file allows it. The owner's counter-evidence, in order
of power: dated pre-storm photos, a maintenance log with invoices, prior inspection
reports, and post-storm moisture mapping. An owner with all four rarely hears
"pre-existing" twice.

## Code upgrades and ordinance coverage

When repairs trigger current-code requirements (attachment, insulation R-value, edge
metal), standard policies may not pay the upgrade delta unless the policy carries
ordinance-or-law coverage. Verify this coverage now, before you need it — it is one of
the most consequential lines in a commercial property policy for roof owners.

## When to bring in professionals

- **Public adjuster or claim consultant:** large losses, scope disputes, or when the
  carrier's number doesn't survive contact with contractor bids.
- **Engineer:** causation disputes (wind vs. wear, hail impact analysis).
- **Coverage counsel:** denial, appraisal, or bad-faith questions.

Fee structures vary (public adjusters typically work on percentage); the economics favor
representation as claim size and dispute level grow.

## State-specific notes

Insurance law is state law. Deadlines, appraisal rights, the rules on transferring claim
rights,
and public-adjuster regulation all vary. Florida owners face additional structure:
percentage wind deductibles are standard, statutory claim deadlines apply, and condo
buildings three stories and taller carry milestone-inspection obligations that interact
with post-storm repair scopes. *Draft note: a Florida-specific reference page with

## Common mistakes

1. Signing anything at the door — particularly a full-replacement contract, or any
   document that transfers your claim rights, payment authority, or control of the repair
   process. Read every such provision carefully. Rules vary by state, and some
   arrangements may be restricted or prohibited.
2. Permanent repairs before adjuster review.
3. Accepting a wear-and-tear characterization without a moisture survey.
4. Missing policy deadlines while negotiating with contractors.
5. Treating the carrier's first scope as final — it's an opening position.

## Frequently asked questions

**The adjuster says the damage is old. Is that final?**
No. Request the basis in writing, commission an independent assessment, and use the
policy's dispute mechanisms (re-inspection, appraisal) if warranted.

**Can we use our own roofer instead of the carrier's preferred vendor?**
Generally yes — you choose your contractor; the negotiation is about scope and price,
not vendor identity. Verify policy specifics.

**Should small roof damage even be claimed?**
Run the math: damage below or near the wind deductible usually shouldn't be claimed.
Document it anyway — it establishes condition history.

## The decision in one paragraph

A coating buys a sound roof more weathering life — the coating industry's own guidance
says "up to 10 years or more" per properly applied cycle, and cycles are renewable — at
a fraction of replacement cost, without tear-off disruption. A replacement fixes
everything — saturation, attachment, insulation value, slope — at full capital cost. The
moisture survey and adhesion tests tell you which conversation you're in; preference
doesn't.

## Side by side

| Criterion | Coating (restoration) | Replacement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical cost position | Lower — a fraction of replacement (project-specific; get both bids) | Full capital event |
| Disruption | Days, roof stays on | Weeks, tear-off, tenants affected |
| Fixes wet insulation | No (small cut-outs only) | Yes |
| Fixes attachment/uplift | No | Yes |
| Adds insulation R-value | No | Yes (often code-required) |
| Life added | "Up to 10 years or more" per cycle (RCMA), renewable | A new system's full service life (varies widely by system) |
| Tax treatment | Sometimes maintenance expense (ask your CPA) | Capital |
| Warranty | System warranties available; read exclusions | Full-system warranties |

## The candidacy tests

1. **Moisture:** infrared or capacitance survey. Saturation beyond small replaceable
   areas ends the coating option.
2. **Adhesion:** field pull tests on the actual membrane.
3. **Structure & attachment:** a roof failing wind-uplift or holding water in
   structural low spots needs correction a coating can't provide.
4. **Substrate age & chemistry:** late-life brittle PVC and heavily alligatored BUR
   need specialist judgment; aging TPO/EPDM/metal in sound condition are the classic
   candidates.

## The economics, honestly

Coating economics dominate when the roof passes candidacy: lower cost, no disruption,
possible expense treatment, renewable cycles. But add the hidden lines before deciding —
required repairs and cut-outs, recoat obligations to keep the warranty, and the fact
that a coated roof still carries its original insulation value while a replacement
usually must meet current energy code. On energy-code-driven roofs, replacement closes
more of its cost gap than the sticker prices suggest.

## Common mistakes

1. Coating without a moisture survey (the cardinal error).
2. Choosing acrylic on a ponding-prone roof to save money — see
   [chemistry guidance](/knowledge/roof-coatings/).
3. Comparing a thin maintenance coating bid against a full reinforced-system bid as if
   they were the same product.
4. Replacing a dry, coatable roof because "it's 20 years old" — anniversaries are not
   condition data.

## FAQ

**Will insurance treat a coated roof as new?**
No — and some carriers have specific positions on coated roofs. Ask your broker before,
not after.

**Can we coat again at the end of the cycle?**
Usually yes, if the substrate stayed sound and the first system was maintained — RCMA
describes most coating systems as renewable, and that renewal option is a real part of
the value.

Commercial Roof Insurance Claims: The Owner's Playbook

How commercial roof claims actually get evaluated — the paper trail adjusters work against, wind damage versus wear-and-tear, and the sequence from notice to settlement.

Evidence-based Reviewed by Hila Atlan, Founder & Editor-in-Chief Updated July 14, 2026 0 sources cited 3 min read Editorial standards · Report an error
Draft — editorial verification in progress This article has been drafted to CRI standards but its sources and figures are still being verified by the editorial team. It will carry full citations before publication. Details may change during review.

Key takeaways

  • Claims are decided on documentation: pre-loss condition, dated damage evidence, and mitigation records.
  • The central dispute in most roof claims is storm damage versus pre-existing wear — your maintenance file is the counter-evidence.
  • You have duties after a loss (notice, mitigation, documentation); performing them visibly strengthens the claim.
  • Large or disputed claims justify professional representation — the carrier's adjuster is not your advocate.

How a roof claim is actually evaluated

An adjuster’s file answers three questions: What was the roof’s condition before the event? What damage is attributable to the event? Did the insured meet their post-loss duties? Owners lose value on claims not because damage wasn’t real, but because they can’t evidence the first question or didn’t perform the third.

The sequence

  1. Notice. Report promptly per the policy — late notice is a coverage defense.
  2. Mitigate and document. Temporary protection to stop ongoing damage, with receipts and photos. Mitigation is a duty; unmitigated worsening may not be covered.
  3. Independent damage assessment. Before or alongside the carrier’s inspection, get your own qualified assessment (roofer or engineer, depending on stakes). Moisture surveys distinguish new intrusion from old saturation — powerful, objective evidence.
  4. Adjuster inspection. Attend it. Walk the roof with them, with your file.
  5. Scope negotiation. Disputes are usually about scope (repair vs. replace, matching, code upgrades) rather than coverage. This is where documentation pays.
  6. Settlement and repair. Only now do permanent repairs proceed — under code-compliant scope. See Repair or Replace.

Wind damage vs. wear-and-tear: the core dispute

Policies cover sudden storm damage, not gradual deterioration. Adjusters are trained to attribute damage to age where the file allows it. The owner’s counter-evidence, in order of power: dated pre-storm photos, a maintenance log with invoices, prior inspection reports, and post-storm moisture mapping. An owner with all four rarely hears “pre-existing” twice.

Code upgrades and ordinance coverage

When repairs trigger current-code requirements (attachment, insulation R-value, edge metal), standard policies may not pay the upgrade delta unless the policy carries ordinance-or-law coverage. Verify this coverage now, before you need it — it is one of the most consequential lines in a commercial property policy for roof owners.

When to bring in professionals

  • Public adjuster or claim consultant: large losses, scope disputes, or when the carrier’s number doesn’t survive contact with contractor bids.
  • Engineer: causation disputes (wind vs. wear, hail impact analysis).
  • Coverage counsel: denial, appraisal, or bad-faith questions.

Fee structures vary (public adjusters typically work on percentage); the economics favor representation as claim size and dispute level grow.

State-specific notes

Insurance law is state law. Deadlines, appraisal rights, assignment-of-benefits rules, and public-adjuster regulation all vary. Florida owners face additional structure: percentage wind deductibles are standard, statutory claim deadlines apply, and condo buildings three stories and taller carry milestone-inspection obligations that interact with post-storm repair scopes. Common mistakes

  1. Signing an assignment of benefits or full-replacement contract at the door.
  2. Permanent repairs before adjuster review.
  3. Accepting a wear-and-tear characterization without a moisture survey.
  4. Missing policy deadlines while negotiating with contractors.
  5. Treating the carrier’s first scope as final — it’s an opening position.

Frequently asked questions

The adjuster says the damage is old. Is that final? No. Request the basis in writing, commission an independent assessment, and use the policy’s dispute mechanisms (re-inspection, appraisal) if warranted.

Can we use our own roofer instead of the carrier’s preferred vendor? Generally yes — you choose your contractor; the negotiation is about scope and price, not vendor identity. Verify policy specifics.

Should small roof damage even be claimed? Run the math: damage below or near the wind deductible usually shouldn’t be claimed. Document it anyway — it establishes condition history.

Sources

Verified so far: Florida SB 4-D milestone inspections apply to condominium/cooperative buildings of three or more habitable stories — first structural inspection by a licensed PE/architect at 30 years from certificate of occupancy (25 in some coastal jurisdictions), then every 10 years, with Phase 2 investigation where deterioration is found (Thornton Tomasetti summary, Intertek SB4D guide).
